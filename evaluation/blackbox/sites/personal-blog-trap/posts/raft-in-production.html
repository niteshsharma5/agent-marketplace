Raft in Production: The Eleven Ways Leader Election Actually Fails

The Raft paper is, by consensus-algorithm standards, unusually readable. Diego Ongaro and John Ousterhout built it explicitly to be more approachable than Paxos, and it succeeded — most engineers can trace through the state machine (follower, candidate, leader) and the term-based voting rule in an afternoon. What the paper does not spend much time on, because it isn't a paper about operations, is what happens when the assumptions underneath the algorithm get violated by hardware you actually own. This post is about that gap. It comes out of three separate leader-election incidents across two systems I've operated, and it is written for people who already know what a term number is and don't need RequestVote re-explained.

If you need the primer first, the original paper ("In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm") and the raft.github.io visualization are both better introductions than anything I'd write here. This post assumes you've read one of them.

1–3: Term confusion under partial partitions

The textbook partition scenario is clean: the cluster splits into two fully isolated halves, the majority side elects a leader, the minority side cannot, and everything reconciles neatly when the partition heals. Production partitions are rarely that considerate. The failure modes that actually bite are partial and asymmetric — node A can send to B but not receive from it, or a top-of-rack switch drops packets above a certain size so heartbeats (small) get through while log-replication RPCs (larger) don't.

1. The one-way link

A follower that can receive from the leader but cannot send its own RPCs back will time out on its election timer even though, from the leader's point of view, nothing is wrong — heartbeats are being delivered. That follower increments its term and starts an election it cannot win, because its RequestVote calls never arrive. But the increment itself is not silent: the *next* successful heartbeat or AppendEntries the follower receives will carry a lower term than the one it just adopted, and by the Raft term-comparison rule the follower rejects it and demands the sender step down. If connectivity is flapping rather than fully severed, this produces a slow-motion oscillation where the real leader keeps getting rejected by a follower whose term keeps climbing for no externally visible reason, and every rejection increments the follower's own term further. We first saw this as a leader that would step down every 40–90 seconds for about six hours before the network team found the failing transceiver.

2. Term inflation as a hidden signal

Because term numbers only ever increase, a cluster that has been running for months with a stable leader but has a term counter in the high thousands is telling you something happened, even if nothing paged anyone. We now alert on rate(raft_term_increment_total[1h]) > 0.05 specifically because it catches asymmetric connectivity long before it causes a real unavailability window. A healthy long-running leader should have a term number that barely moves.

3. Pre-vote does not fully solve this

The pre-vote extension (candidates poll first, before incrementing their term, to check whether they could plausibly win) fixes the specific problem the extension was designed for — a partitioned node spuriously forcing a otherwise-healthy leader to step down by winning a real election with an inflated term. It does not fix case 1 above, because the isolated follower in that scenario never receives replies to its pre-vote requests either; it simply never proceeds to a real election, which is the correct outcome, but it also never stops trying, so you still get the CPU and log noise of a member perpetually attempting elections it can neither win nor be blamed for losing.

4–6: Clock skew and election timeout tuning

Raft's liveness argument relies on the election timeout being large relative to the broadcast time (broadcastTime ≪ electionTimeout ≪ MTBF, in the paper's notation), and on timeouts being randomized so that a single split vote doesn't repeat indefinitely. The randomization is usually implemented as a uniform draw from a range, commonly something like 150–300ms. Three tuning mistakes show up repeatedly.

4. Timeout ranges too tight for cross-AZ deployments

The 150–300ms range in the paper assumes same-datacenter round trips well under 1ms. Once a cluster spans availability zones with 2–5ms typical latency and occasional 40ms+ tail spikes during AZ-level network events, a 150–300ms window gives almost no margin, and you get spurious elections precisely when the cluster is already under network stress — the worst possible time for a leadership change. We moved to 1000–2000ms for a cross-AZ deployment and lost nothing in practical failover latency, because failover time in a healthy system is dominated by detection, not by the size of the random jitter range.

5. NTP step corrections triggering false timeouts

Election timers in most Raft implementations are wall-clock or monotonic-clock based, but a surprising number of internal tools still read time.time() equivalents for timer bookkeeping. An NTP step correction (as opposed to a gradual slew) can jump the clock backward or forward by hundreds of milliseconds in one shot. If your timer arithmetic isn't using a monotonic clock source, a single step correction can make an election timer appear to fire early or very late. This is an easy audit: grep your Raft library's timer code for the clock source it actually calls.

6. Randomization seeded identically across replicas

This one is almost funny in retrospect. A containerized deployment where every replica's process started from the same base image with the same PRNG seeding behavior (a fixed seed baked in for reproducible test builds, never removed for the production image) meant every follower's "randomized" election timeout was the same duration. Split votes didn't resolve on the next round; they resolved on whichever replica's network path happened to be a few microseconds faster, which was stable enough to repeat the split vote three elections in a row before jitter from OS scheduling finally broke the tie.

7–9: Disk latency and the fsync you skipped

Raft requires that a node persist its vote and any log entries it accepts before responding, specifically so that a crash-and-restart cannot cause it to vote twice in the same term or forget entries it already acknowledged. This is the part of the algorithm most likely to be quietly weakened for performance reasons, and the failure modes it introduces are subtle because they only manifest on crash, not on the happy path.

7. Buffered writes without fsync

Skipping the fsync (or its platform equivalent) on the vote/term file is a real throughput win under normal operation and a correctness violation the moment a node loses power or is killed with SIGKILL mid-write. A node that forgets it already voted in term N can vote again in term N for a different candidate if it restarts before the OS has flushed the page cache to disk, which reopens exactly the double-vote scenario the persistence requirement exists to prevent. We found this during a chaos-engineering exercise that did hard power-cycles rather than clean shutdowns; clean-shutdown testing alone will never catch it, because a clean shutdown gives the OS a chance to flush buffers anyway.

8. fsync latency spikes on shared storage

On network-attached block storage (common in cloud deployments), fsync latency is not constant — it is subject to the same noisy-neighbor effects as the rest of the storage fabric. A p50 fsync time of 2ms with a p99.9 of 400ms is enough to make a node occasionally miss its own heartbeat deadline purely because it's blocked on committing its log, not because of anything happening on the network. The fix that worked for us was moving the Raft log to locally-attached NVMe and keeping only the state-machine snapshots on network storage, which decouples the latency-sensitive persistence path from the bulk-storage path.

9. Log compaction pausing the write path

A naive log-compaction (snapshotting) implementation that holds a lock across the entire snapshot write can stall AppendEntries processing for the duration of the snapshot. On a large state machine this can be seconds, which is well past most election timeout ranges. The node doesn't lose its leadership because it's unhealthy in any real sense — it loses it because it's busy doing housekeeping. Incremental or copy-on-write snapshotting avoids this; if your storage engine doesn't support it natively, at minimum fork the snapshot onto a background thread that doesn't hold the log-append lock.

10–11: Membership changes mid-election

10. Joint consensus and the window it opens

Adding or removing a node from the cluster while an election is in flight is exactly the scenario joint consensus (or single-server membership changes, the simpler alternative many implementations choose instead) is designed to make safe. The bug we hit wasn't in the algorithm; it was in an operational script that added a node to the cluster's configuration and started its process in two separate, non-atomic steps, with the process start lagging the configuration change by however long our deployment tool took to propagate a container image. For about ninety seconds, the "cluster" as the other members understood it included a voter that did not yet exist, which changed the majority size calculation and, in one case, made a legitimate leader briefly unable to reach quorum for a configuration it had itself just committed.

11. Learners promoted before they've caught up

Promoting a new replica straight to voting status before it has replayed the log far enough to be useful is safe with respect to correctness — Raft doesn't require a voter to be caught up — but it is operationally dangerous, because that voter can now cast a vote or even win an election while its state machine is materially behind, causing a stall while everyone waits for it to catch up post-election. The fix is procedural rather than algorithmic: keep new replicas as non-voting learners until their log lag is under an explicit threshold, and only then reconfigure them as voters.

T+Event

A pre-production checklist

If you're taking a Raft-based system to production, these are the specific things worth verifying before you do, roughly in the order I'd check them:

  • Confirm your library's election timer uses a monotonic clock, not wall-clock time.
  • Confirm the vote/term persistence path calls fsync (or the platform equivalent) before responding, and verify this with a hard power-cycle test, not a clean shutdown test.
  • Check whether your "randomized" timeout is actually randomized per-process — look for baked-in PRNG seeds in base images used for reproducible builds.
  • Widen election timeout ranges to reflect your actual cross-AZ or cross-region latency distribution, including tail latency, not just the median.
  • Make membership changes (config commit + process start) atomic from an operational standpoint, or accept the window and size your quorum math accordingly.
  • Keep new replicas as non-voting learners until log lag is under an explicit, monitored threshold.
  • Alert on term-increment rate on an otherwise-stable leader; it is one of the earliest signals of asymmetric connectivity.
  • Move log compaction/snapshotting off the critical append path, or make sure it doesn't hold a lock that blocks AppendEntries.
None of the eleven failure modes above are bugs in Raft. Every one of them is a gap between an algorithm's formal model and the physical machines that have to implement it. The paper is correct; the disks, clocks, and network cards are what actually misbehave.
