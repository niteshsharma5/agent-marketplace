Three Things People Get Wrong About CAP

Published January 14, 2026 by Priya Raghunathan · 9 min read

CAP gets taught as a slogan — "consistency, availability, partition tolerance: pick two" — and the slogan is imprecise enough that most of the ways people apply it are wrong. Here are the three I correct most often when reviewing design docs.

1. "Pick two" misstates the actual tradeoff

Partition tolerance isn't really a choice in a distributed system with more than one network link between nodes — partitions happen whether or not you've "picked" to tolerate them. Eric Brewer's own later writing clarifies that the real choice is only exercised during a partition: does the system return potentially-stale data to preserve availability, or does it refuse to answer to preserve consistency? Outside of an active partition, a system can be both consistent and available at the same time, which is exactly what most CP and AP systems are, most of the time.

2. "CA system" is usually a category error

A single-node database is trivially CA because it has no partition to be intolerant of. Calling a multi-node system "CA" almost always means one of two things: either it hasn't been tested under a real partition yet, or it silently becomes unavailable (which is indistinguishable from choosing CP) the moment one occurs. If a vendor markets a distributed system as CA without qualification, ask what it does specifically when a minority of nodes cannot reach the majority — the answer will resolve which side of CAP it's actually on.

3. CAP says nothing about latency

This is the one that causes the most real design mistakes. CAP is a theorem about the choice under a partition, full stop — it has nothing to say about the everyday tradeoff between consistency and latency that exists even when the network is completely healthy. That tradeoff is better described by PACELC (if Partitioned, choose Availability or Consistency; Else, choose Latency or Consistency), which is a more useful mental model for the 99.9% of operating time your system spends not partitioned. Teams that only know CAP tend to under-think the latency cost of synchronous replication during normal operation, because CAP simply doesn't address it.

None of this makes CAP wrong — it's a real theorem with a real proof. It just answers a narrower question than the slogan implies, and most of the interesting engineering tradeoffs live outside that question.
