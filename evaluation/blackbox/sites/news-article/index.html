Utilities Are Quietly Betting Summer Peaks on Batteries, Not Gas Peakers

Filings from five regional grid operators show battery fleets, not new gas plants, are now the default answer to the hottest afternoon hours of the year.

By Priya Chandran, Senior Energy Correspondent

Published August 29, 2026, 12:12 PM ET · Updated August 30, 2026, 9:05 AM ET · 6 min read

For most of the last two decades, utilities facing a scorching afternoon reached for the same tool: a gas-fired "peaker" plant that could be switched on for a few hours and switched off again once demand fell. This summer, in filings reviewed by The Continental Ledger, five regional grid operators described a different default — battery storage that charges overnight on cheap power and discharges during the four-to-six-hour window when air conditioning load peaks.

The shift is not experimental. According to interconnection data published by the operators themselves, battery capacity committed to peak-shaving duty in those five regions grew from roughly 4.1 gigawatts in 2023 to just over 14 gigawatts this year — enough to cover the incremental afternoon demand that would otherwise have required new gas capacity.

Why batteries won the peak-hour argument

Three factors explain the change, according to filings and interviews with two grid-planning engineers who reviewed the data at the Ledger's request. First, battery costs have fallen enough that a four-hour storage system now competes directly with a new peaker on a per-megawatt basis. Second, batteries can respond in seconds rather than the ten-to-twenty minutes a gas turbine needs to spin up, which matters more as operators manage sharper, shorter demand spikes. Third, permitting a battery site has generally moved faster than permitting a new combustion turbine, particularly in areas with tightening air-quality rules.

"We used to plan the summer around how many peakers we could get built in time," said one of the engineers, who works for a Southwestern grid operator and asked not to be named because the filings are still under regulatory review. "Now the planning conversation is about how many hours of storage we can string together before the sun goes down."

The limits of the battery bet

The trend has a ceiling. Four-hour batteries are well suited to the classic late-afternoon peak, but planners in three of the five regions flagged a harder problem: multi-day heat events where overnight temperatures stay high enough that batteries never fully recharge before the next afternoon's peak arrives. In those stretches, gas peakers and demand-response programs still do the heavy lifting.

A related article examines how one Texas cooperative's battery fleet performed during exactly that kind of extended heat dome last month — see "How a Texas Co-op's Battery Fleet Survived the August Heat Dome."

What happens next

Two of the five operators say they expect battery capacity dedicated to peak coverage to roughly double again by 2028, based on projects already in their interconnection queues. Whether that pace holds depends heavily on transmission buildout and on how quickly component costs continue to fall — both points the Ledger will track in future coverage.

Correction, August 30, 2026: An earlier version of this article misstated the 2023 battery capacity figure as 3.1 gigawatts. It was 4.1 gigawatts. The figure has been corrected.
